A note from Christopher Hayes

Why I created this

My aim is to keep learning about AI and share what I learn in a form people can explore and use. I created this experience to make the choices inside an AI task easier to see: what was requested, what the system did and where permission mattered.

Whether you use AI, build with it or lead a team, I want this to help you ask better questions before giving it more responsibility.

You delegated a task.
Which decisions went with it?

How this will help

Three things to take into your own work

  1. Spot the consequential choice.Recognize when a workaround changes who can access information, what a system may do or who needs to approve.
  2. Judge what the evidence supports.Distinguish an attempted action from a completed one, and a reported response from a proven fix.
  3. Set clearer boundaries.Practise defining what an agent may decide, when it should pause and what evidence you need to review.

No technical background needed. Follow one story, explore the patterns and try a delegation decision yourself. Authority means who is allowed to decide what.

What we are learning from: 29 selected public reports and notices published by OpenAI, including training examples, experiments and investigations. These accounts help us examine decisions; they do not tell us how often these behaviours occur.

About 9 minutes for the story. Read the main narrative first; expand explanations or inspect any case when you want more depth.

New to this topic? Three useful terms
Agent
An AI system that can use tools and take steps toward a task, such as creating a workbook.
Delegation
Giving a task to someone or something, together with permission to make specified decisions.
Disclosure
A published account. It may describe an incident, an experiment, an investigation or behaviour judged acceptable.

Why these five, and what do the markers mean?

The examples contrast preparation without execution, an executed upload, fabricated inputs followed by rejection, simulated action, and a supported action judged non-misaligned. They are selected to teach distinctions, not rank risks.

Prepared
The action was made ready, for example by writing a script or configuring a supported command. A thought alone is not treated as execution.
Attempted / executed
Starting an action and establishing that it occurred are separate questions. Execution means the named action in its stated setting, including a simulation where explicitly labelled.
Not established
The report does not establish the named outcome. This does not mean it did not occur.
Not applicable
The outcome is outside that example’s scope, such as live delivery in a simulated test.

The final column asks a different, named outcome question in each row. It is not a common impact scale. Checkpoint coding was reviewed against the five linked sources on 21 November 2026. What could this mean for you?

Optional perspectives · Illustrative applications, not additional incidents.

If you use AI

Say what may change and who may receive the result. For a shared document, distinguish drafting it from publishing it. If a task stalls, agree on a permitted fallback or an occasion to ask.

Ask: “Which choices am I delegating along with this task?”

If you deploy AI

Give the agent a workable approved route and a clear way to stop or escalate. Test what happens when files, tools or services are unavailable. Keep a record of attempts as well as completed actions.

Ask: “Can our permissions and logs enforce—and show—the boundary we described?”

If you lead a team

Name who can approve a change of method, recipient or scope. Define success as a useful outcome reached within that authority; examine failed attempts and consequences separately.

Illustrative wealth management application: a dealer might allow an agent to draft a client-meeting summary while reserving changes to client records and external sharing for separately authorized actions.

Ask: “Who owns the decision when the planned route stops working?”

Three questions to take into your next task

  1. What may the agent decide?Name the permitted actions, recipients and methods.
  2. When must it ask?Define what happens when a route fails or the scope changes.
  3. How will we know what happened?Distinguish what was prepared, attempted, executed and verified.

The workbook needed a route to its collaborators. Choosing that route also meant choosing who could access it. Make that decision part of the delegation.

What this collection contains

The register captured 26 reports and 3 notices on 18 November 2026. Our authority themes were reviewed on 19 November. The five checkpoint examples and 26 report pages were checked on 21 November. The new authority comparison uses those reports and the collected notice accounts. The dated notice detail remains at the original snapshot. The collection is a research snapshot, not a live incident feed.

How to read the visualizations

The atlas uses equal-sized leaves and fixed category colours. Its six authority themes are analyst classifications. The unverified notice remains unassigned. The matrix separately codes five teaching examples against their source narratives. Its stages are not severity levels; its final outcomes differ by row.

The time axis uses recorded dates and date ranges. Response actions without a recorded date stay in the response panel. Missing timing does not mean an event did not occur.

What the evidence cannot establish

This selected collection does not supply an exposure denominator, incident rate, vendor comparison, or common causal mechanism. Disclosures may describe multiple examples and related events. One disclosure is not necessarily one unique run.

“Reported” means documented by the source, not independently forensically verified by this project. Proposed explanations, response actions, and demonstrated outcomes are kept separate. The optional applications and delegation exercise are design suggestions, not tested controls.

The nine-minute reading guide is an editorial estimate, assuming about 200 words per minute for the core story and default evidence explanations. It excludes optional explanations and source inspection. It is not a timed reader study; visual exploration takes additional time.

      Design approach

      Angle: examine the decisions an agent takes when completing work. Framing: a selected disclosure collection with explicit limits. Focus: one consequential decision at a time. This is our application of Andy Kirk’s angle, framing, and focus approach.

      Proximity keeps claims beside their limits; connected branches show grouping; repeated marker shapes make evidence states comparable. Colour identifies themes and directs attention. It never substitutes for a text label.
